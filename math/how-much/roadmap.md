## How the Unit Unfolds

### Part 1 — Area Means Something (3/31 – 4/10)

You'll start with a speed graph and ask: *how far did you go?*

It turns out the answer is hiding in the **area under the graph**. You'll test this idea across a bunch of different contexts — a dripping faucet, household electricity use — and find the same structure every time. The context changes; the math doesn't.

By the end of Part 1, you should be able to look at any rate graph and extract a total amount from it.

### Part 2 — What If the Graph Is Curved? (4/21 – 4/23)

So far the rate graphs have been made of flat, horizontal pieces — easy to find the area. Now the rate changes continuously, like sunlight hitting a solar panel over the course of a day. You can't get an exact area, but you *can* get a good estimate by breaking the region into pieces.

This is the core idea behind integration — and approximation turns out to be more powerful than it sounds.

### Part 3 — Running It in Reverse (4/27)

Now flip it. You're given a *distance graph* and asked: how fast were you going at a specific moment?

You'll figure this out by looking at smaller and smaller time intervals around that moment. As the intervals shrink, the average speed homes in on the *instantaneous* speed. This process — taking a limit — is the foundation of the derivative.

### Part 4 — Making It Algebraic (4/29 – 5/1)

Finally, you'll move from graphs to formulas. Instead of estimating a derivative from a picture, you'll compute it from an equation. You'll find a pattern that works for all polynomial functions — the **power rule** — so you don't have to go through the whole limit process every time.
