## Projects

### Intro / Paper Airplane

- This served as the first mini-project to introduce students to the engineering course.
- It was used to rapidly practice the full Engineering Design Process cycle: Ask, Research, Imagine, Plan, Create, Test, and Improve.
- The project involved identifying constraints and requirements (such as distance and speed), brainstorming, creating sketches, constructing the plane, and testing measurements against requirements.

### Bean Bag Launcher

- **Objective:** Design a mini bean bag launcher with the goal of launching a 6-gram bean bag as far as possible.
- **Materials:** 2 popsicle sticks, 1 plastic spoon, 2 small rubber bands, 1 giant rubber band, a small amount of hot glue, masking tape, and cardboard.
- **Requirements:** The only rule during testing is that you can only hold the base on the ground.
- **Performance Metric (Distance):** Reaching less than 2 meters is a 1; 2–4 meters is a 2; 4–5 meters is a 3; and more than 5 meters is a 4.
- **Key Physics Concepts:** Explored elastic potential energy converting entirely into kinetic energy, and how an optimal launch occurs when angled at 45 degrees.

### Foam Glider

- **Objective:** Explore aerospace engineering by answering the essential questions of what forces act on a glider and how to minimize drag while maximizing lift.
- **Key Science Concepts:** Used Free Body Diagrams to look at force vectors (pushes and pulls), noting that equal/opposite forces cancel out and applying the equation Force = mass × acceleration.
- **Initial Prototype Metric:** A score of 1 means the glider is not constructed; a 2 means it can be thrown but does not fly straight; a 3 means it flies straight; and a 4 means it flies greater than 3 meters.
- **Final Prototype Metric:** Flying less than 3 meters is a 1; flying between 3 and 5–7 meters is a 2; flying 5–9 meters is a 3; and flying 10 meters is a 4.

### Agrivoltaic/Model Farm

### Bridges

- **Unit Essential Question:** What materials and structures can we use to build the bridge that will hold the most weight?

#### Lego Bridge

- **Type:** Built as a beam bridge structure.
- **Performance Scale:** Evaluated based on how much weight the structure can support perpendicular to the table. Supporting no weight is a 1; supporting weight less than 1 gallon of water is a 2; holding exactly 1 gallon of water is a 3; holding 2 gallons of water is a 3.5; and supporting 25 lbs of weight is a 4.

#### Spaghetti Marshmallow/Truss Bridge

- **Objective:** Build a truss bridge to learn why triangles are such a strong shape compared to squares when subjected to down-force weight.
- **Class Activity Constraints:** Built in pairs starting out with 10 marshmallows and 10 pieces of spaghetti. The bridge must successfully span 30 cm and hold weight for 10 seconds. Standing up by itself is a 2; holding 2 pairs of scissors is a 3; holding 3 pairs of scissors is a 4.
- **3D Modeling Extension:** Designed a truss bridge in TinkerCAD to be 3D printed and tested. The goal is to minimize filament use and maximize structural strength from the truss geometry. The 3D printer constraints limit the bridge length to 24 cm.

#### Suspension Bridge

- **Objective:** Investigate what elements make a suspension bridge capable of holding the largest amount of weight.
- **Materials:** Fishing wire, popsicle sticks, cardboard, Lego (strictly restricted for use only in building the towers), hot glue, and tape.
- **Requirements & Testing:** The bridge must span 30 cm, and the suspension cables must visibly hold tension. Students are permitted to physically hold the towers during the weight test. Supporting 25 lbs achieves a score of 3, while supporting 50 lbs achieves a 4.
